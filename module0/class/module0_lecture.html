3,336 people.

Pennsylvania recorded 3,336 overdose deaths in 2024

A single state-level count. It is also the population of a small Pennsylvania borough.

The question this session opens

A state total describes no one. Pennsylvania comprises 67 counties whose overdose burdens differ by nearly an order of magnitude, and the state figure conceals that variation entirely.

This session establishes what that number means for a single county. The question is answered at the end, from data, using code written in this room.

Prerequisite assumed: six weeks of R. The concepts of a variable, a data frame, and the distinction between a count and a rate carry over intact.

Open cold. Say the number, then pause before the borough line — it lands better spoken than read. Do not answer the question yet; the payoff slide at the end is where Schuylkill arrives.

For the local comparison, pick whichever your room will feel: 3,336 is roughly the population of Jim Thorpe, or roughly a third of a typical Pitt first-year class.

Say the prerequisite line out loud and mean it. This room is not full of beginners — they have six weeks of R behind them, and the whole module runs faster if you tell them you know that in the first two minutes. Something like: “You already know the hard part. What you don’t know yet is where Python puts things.”

The data has a named source and a documented limitation

Every dataset in this course traces to a public agency, and every limitation is stated.

Source Pennsylvania Department of Health, Estimated Drug Overdose Deaths
Coverage 67 counties, 2019–2024
Definition Accidental and undetermined drug overdose deaths
Known limitation PA DOH marks 2024 provisional — death certificates lag three to six months

The apparent decline in 2024 is therefore partly a real change and partly incomplete reporting. Both facts are stated rather than reconciled.

Two readings of the same value

3336 is a quantity admitting arithmetic. It is also 3,336 deaths. Neither reading displaces the other, and this course maintains both.

This slide sets the honesty standard for the course, so do not rush it.

On provisional data: students will notice 2024 looks better than 2023 and will want to call it good news. Ask them whether they believe it. The honest answer is “partly, and partly we don’t know yet” — and getting comfortable saying that is a real public-health skill.

On the two readings: say it once, here, plainly, and then let it go. If you repeat “the numbers are people” every module it becomes a slogan and stops meaning anything. Gravity, not sensation.

The objective is analytic, not vocational

The purpose of this course is not programming proficiency as an end in itself.

The purpose is to pose a question about a population and obtain a defensible answer from data. Code is instrumental.

Prior R coursework transfers directly

Six weeks of R established the conceptual apparatus: what a variable holds, why grouping precedes summarizing, why a rate supports comparison and a count does not. Python supplies a second implementation of familiar ideas.

Where a Python construct has a direct R analogue, this course notes the correspondence in one line and proceeds.

This is where you set expectations for pace. Say it directly: “You’re not here to become programmers, and I’m not going to teach this like a CS course, because you’re not CS students.”

The bridge callouts are deliberately one line each. Do not build a comparison table between R and Python — it doubles the vocabulary load and teaches neither language well. If someone asks a detailed “but in R you’d…” question, answer it in ten seconds and move on.

Four criteria determine marks: correctness and reproducibility of code, quality of interpretation, presentation — style, readability, grammar, spelling — and effective integration of computer output into written work.

Reproducibility is assessed explicitly

Code that produced a result once and cannot produce it again constitutes incomplete work. Module 6 is organized around this criterion; it applies from the first submission.

Flag the grammar-and-spelling criterion out loud. It’s in the syllabus, students routinely assume it’s boilerplate, and it isn’t — presentation quality is a named criterion and it does move grades.

Reproducibility gets its own callout because it’s the one criterion with no R analogue in their heads yet. Give them the concrete version: “If you restart your notebook and run every cell top to bottom, does it still produce your report? If not, it isn’t finished.” In module 6 that becomes an actual button they press.

Generative AI is not permitted in this course

The prohibition covers labs, homework, and the final project.

The rationale is specific. The assessed outcome is the student’s own capability. A model that produces working code yields a submission that executes and an analyst who cannot diagnose it. The skill is acquired during the six weeks it takes to acquire it.

The official environment ships an AI assistant enabled by default

Google Colab includes Gemini. Disabling it is a required setup step, documented on the following slide, and takes approximately fifteen seconds.

Substitutes: office hours, lab partners, the class channel, the instructor.

Give the reason once, without an academic-integrity lecture. Undergraduates respond far better to “here’s why this rule exists” than to a threat, and the reason here is honestly a good one. Say something like: “I’d rather you submit something broken that you understand than something perfect that you don’t.”

Be candid about the awkward part — the university’s official environment ships an AI assistant switched on, and we’re asking them to turn it off. Naming that costs nothing and buys you credibility for the rest of the policy.

The substitutes line matters. Say you mean it, and say when your office hours are.

Colab.

Colab provides a browser-based notebook executing on Google’s infrastructure. No local installation is required.

Access at colab.research.google.com using a Pitt account.

Do this live on the projector instead of reading the table. Two minutes, screen shared, and have them follow along on their own machines.

The “save a copy” warning is the single highest-value sentence in this section. It happens every term without exception. Tell them so: “Somebody in this room is going to lose an hour of lab work to this. It may as well be somebody who was warned.”

Anaconda is documented on Canvas as an optional local setup. Don’t spend class time on it — Colab is official and everything graded runs there.

Disabling Gemini is a required setup step

Tools > Settings > AI Assistance — disable both settings:

  • Show AI-powered code completions
  • Show generative AI features

Inline completion bypasses the decision being assessed

Accepting a suggested line is not an authored line. Independent of intent, the result is submitted code the student did not choose. Disabling the feature eliminates the ambiguity.

These settings can revert after a Colab update and warrant periodic verification.

Do this live as well, and ask for hands up when both toggles are off. Thirty seconds, and it’s the only way to know the room actually did it rather than nodding.

The distinction worth drawing out loud: this isn’t mainly about cheating. It’s that inline completion removes the moment where you decide what the code should do — and that moment is the entire skill. Say that; it lands better than the policy framing.

A notebook preserves execution state, not screen order

Notebooks contain code cells and text cells. Shift+Enter executes a cell and advances.

The consequential property: the interpreter retains what was executed, not what is displayed.

population = 143786      # executed first

population = 157795      # executed second

# The first cell is then edited to 999999 and NOT re-executed.
# The value of population remains 157795.

Execution order is invocation order

Execution does not proceed top to bottom. The bracketed number beside each cell records the true sequence. This accounts for roughly half of all inexplicable notebook behavior.

Resolution: Runtime > Restart and run all, which is also the test of whether a notebook is reproducible.

This is the most important idea in module 0 and the one with no R-script analogue — a sourced R script runs top to bottom, always. A notebook does not.

Do the worked example live: run three cells, go back and change the first, don’t re-run it, then print. Ask the room what it will print before you press it. Enough people guess wrong that the point lands hard, and it’s much better learned from a wrong guess than from a slide.

Connect it forward explicitly: “restart and run all” is what reproducibility means in practice, and it’s 30% of the grade in module 6.

Two mechanisms transfer files into Colab

Upload — the folder icon in the left sidebar, then the upload control. Immediate, but the file is discarded when the runtime disconnects.

Drive mount — persistent, and the preferred mechanism:

from google.colab import drive
drive.mount('/content/drive')

A mounted file is then addressed by path:

'/content/drive/MyDrive/pubhlt0411/pa_overdose_county_year.csv'

Uploaded files do not persist

Runtime disconnection discards uploaded files. Mounting Drive establishes a stable path across sessions.

Demonstrate the mount live. The permissions pop-up is confusing the first time and much less confusing once they’ve watched somebody click through it.

Practical instruction: have them make a pubhlt0411 folder in Drive today and drop the overdose CSV into it. Every later module assumes there’s somewhere to put a file.

Worth saying: re-uploading the same CSV four times in one lab is a rite of passage, and you’d rather they skip it.

Note the slides they’re looking at run Python in the browser instead — no Colab, no upload. That’s a teaching convenience for lecture. Graded work happens in Colab.

Values.

Assignment binds a name to a value

The = operator does not assert equality. It binds: the expression on the right is evaluated, and the name on the left refers to the result.

The statement is read right to left: construct the value, then attach the name.

R analogue

county <- "Schuylkill". The semantics are equivalent. Python uses = and provides no <-.

Say the right-to-left reading out loud — it’s worth thirty seconds. Students who read = as “equals” get stuck in module 2 on count = count + 1, which is nonsense as an equation and obvious as an instruction.

These are the real 2023 Schuylkill County numbers, and they stay these numbers all semester. When Schuylkill comes back in module 1, it comes back with the same variable names and the same values. That consistency is deliberate — tell them the stories recur.

Identifier choice is a documentation decision

Constraints: letters, digits, underscores; no spaces; no leading digit. Identifiers are case-sensitive — Population and population denote different names.

Convention: lower_case_with_underscores, not PopulationCount.

Readability is an assessed criterion

Identifier quality falls under presentation, which is a named grading criterion in this course.

Don’t soften this one. The habit forms in the first week or it doesn’t form at all, and the cost of x stays invisible until the notebook is long enough to hurt. Tell them: “x costs you marks here, and it costs your future self twenty minutes.”

R conventions are often dot.separated or camelCase. Python is near-universally snake_case. One line, then move on.

Every value carries a type that determines valid operations

Four types account for nearly all data handled in this course.

type() reports the type of any value and is the appropriate diagnostic when an operation behaves unexpectedly.

R analogue

character, integer, numeric, logical. Python writes True and False, not TRUE and FALSE.

Stress the capital T. TRUE raises a NameError in Python and it’s the single most common import from R muscle memory — they will all do it at least once.

Worked example: ask what type(60) and type(60.0) return. Same number to a human, int and float to Python. That distinction is exactly what produces the division surprise two slides from now, so plant it here.

Exercise: bind one county’s values

Centre County, 2023: seven deaths, population 157,795.

# Seven deaths, written as a whole number without quotation marks.
# Quoted, the value becomes the string "7", which does not support division.
county = "Centre" overdose_deaths = 7 population = 157795 print(county, overdose_deaths, population) 
county = "Centre"
overdose_deaths = 7
population = 157795

print(county, overdose_deaths, population)

Fill this in with the room rather than letting them work alone. It’s the first {pyodide} cell they touch, and the point is the mechanics of running a cell, not the difficulty of the blank.

Before you type the answer, ask why "7" in quotes would be wrong. Somebody will say “it’s text” — promise them the TypeError slide is two slides away and that they’re about to see exactly what goes wrong.

Centre County is the counterweight to Schuylkill and returns in module 1 as the direct comparison.

Python does not coerce between text and numbers

Scenario: a file supplies "60" as text. Adding eight should yield 68.

TypeError — unsupported operand types

TypeError: can only concatenate str (not "int") to str

The + operator is defined for str + str (concatenation) and for int + int (addition), but not across the two. Rather than infer an intent, the interpreter raises.

Resolution: convert explicitly — int(overdose_deaths) + 8.

Run it broken. Let the traceback appear on the projector and read the last line out loud — they need to learn early that the bottom line of a traceback is the sentence that matters, and that the wall of text above it is mostly noise.

Then fix it live and re-run.

Be candid: everybody hits this one, including you, all semester. Data read from a file arrives as text constantly. Tell them they’ll stop being surprised by it around week three.

Point out that this error is loud, and that loud is a gift — the dangerous errors are the silent ones that hand you a wrong number without complaint. That’s the next slide.

Conversion between types is explicit

int() truncates toward zero

int(41.7) evaluates to 41. The fractional component is discarded rather than rounded. round(41.7) performs rounding and evaluates to 42.

Pause on the 608 line. It is not an error — Python did exactly what it was told and produced a wrong answer in silence. That’s the more dangerous failure mode and this is the first time they see it. Ask them how they’d catch it. The answer is type(), and that they have to be suspicious.

Truncation bites whenever they compute a per-something count. If you want a second example, show int(49.8) returning 49 — that’s the average deaths per county, and losing 0.8 of a person is a real distortion.

Seven arithmetic operators

Three operators warrant particular attention

/ returns a float even when the division is exact. // discards the remainder. Exponentiation is **; the ^ operator denotes bitwise exclusive-or and evaluates without error to an incorrect result when used for powers.

^ is the R import that hurts most. In R, 2^10 is 1024. In Python, 2^10 is 8 — wrong, silent, and plausible-looking enough to survive a code review. Demonstrate it if there’s time; the silence is the whole lesson.

% corresponds to R’s %%. One character, same job. Mention it and move on.

Exercise: compute a rate per 100,000

A rate per 100,000 is the count divided by the population, multiplied by 100,000. Schuylkill County, 2023.

# The multiplier is 100000, written without a comma separator.
# Python parses 100,000 as two values, not one, and the result will not be a rate.
overdose_deaths = 60 population = 143786 rate_per_100k = overdose_deaths / population * 100000 print(rate_per_100k) 
overdose_deaths = 60
population = 143786

rate_per_100k = overdose_deaths / population * 100000

print(rate_per_100k)

The output carries excessive precision

41.728680121847745 is arithmetically correct and unsuitable for presentation. Formatting is addressed on the following slide.

The comma warning in the hint is not hypothetical. 100,000 is legal Python that means something completely different — it builds a tuple — so it fails confusingly rather than cleanly. Somebody will do it.

They have computed a rate in R already. Say so: this is the same arithmetic they already know, and the only new thing is where Python wants the parentheses.

Leave the ugly output on screen. Don’t clean it up — it’s the motivation for the next slide, and they should feel the need before they’re handed the tool.

f-strings interpolate values into text

An f prefix on a string literal causes each {expression} to be evaluated and substituted.

The :.1f format specifier fixes the output at one digit after the decimal point; :.2f gives two.

Format at presentation, retain full precision in computation

Rounding applied at the point of printing does not affect stored values. Rounding applied during computation propagates through every subsequent step.

.1f is the one they’ll use constantly. This course reports rates to one decimal by convention, because a second decimal implies a precision the underlying data doesn’t support — worth saying, since they’ll otherwise copy whatever Python prints.

R analogue: sprintf("%.1f", x) or glue(). One line.

The forward-looking point is real, not theoretical. In module 4 they compute group rates across many counties, and rounding at the wrong step there produces totals that are visibly, embarrassingly wrong.

Comments document rationale, not syntax

Text following # is ignored by the interpreter and addressed to the reader.

Comments are assessed

Interpretation and presentation together account for the majority of the grading criteria. Comments are where analytic reasoning becomes visible to a reader.

The suppression comment is a genuine preview of module 4, where blank-versus-zero becomes an entire teaching case. Forest County has both, in the same column, in the real file — mention that if you want to make it concrete.

The rule to give them out loud: comment the decision, not the syntax. If a line’s what isn’t obvious, the fix is usually a better variable name rather than a comment explaining a bad one.

The state figure resolves into a ninefold difference

Schuylkill County: 41.7 per 100,000. Centre County: 4.4 per 100,000.

Comparable populations. A ninefold difference in rate.

What the state total concealed

3,336 describes Pennsylvania. It resolves into 41.7 in one county and 4.4 in another, and no resident experiences the state average. This is the justification for conducting the remainder of the course at the county level.

This is the payoff. Land it and stop — do not add another point after it.

Say the closing line plainly, out loud: “Two counties, nearly the same size. There’s the gap. You wrote the code that found it, in your first hour of Python.” That sentence is spoken, not printed — it’s the promise from the cold open being paid off, and it works because they did the work.
