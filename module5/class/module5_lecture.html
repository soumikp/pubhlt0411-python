Graphics in Python

Summarising data, numerically or graphically, is a central part of any analysis. Three layers produce figures in Python:

1. matplotlib — the original plotting library. Every figure in Python is ultimately a matplotlib figure.

2. pandas .plot() — a shortcut that calls matplotlib directly from a DataFrame.

3. seaborn — statistical figures built on matplotlib: it takes a DataFrame and column names, and handles grouping, colour, and legends.

This module begins with matplotlib, which every other layer depends on, and then turns to seaborn. Module 6 continues with seaborn’s statistical layers, facets, scales, and themes.

This is the Python counterpart of the “three graphics systems” slide in the R half, and the mapping is close: matplotlib is base R graphics, seaborn is the closest thing Python has to ggplot2, and pandas .plot() sits in between.

The reason to start with matplotlib rather than skip straight to seaborn is practical. seaborn draws on a matplotlib axes, and every time a seaborn figure needs a title, a label, a tick, or a saved file, the call that does it is a matplotlib call. Learning seaborn without matplotlib means being stuck the first time a default needs changing.

If someone has used ggplot2 in the R half and asks whether Python has a grammar-of-graphics library: yes, seaborn.objects, and also plotnine, which copies ggplot2 almost line for line. Neither is what most Python code in the wild uses, which is why the course teaches the function interface.

The asthma data

Source: CDC PLACES 2024, model-based estimates for adults, all 67 Pennsylvania counties.

Adult asthma prevalence runs from 9.7% to 11.6%. The entire state fits inside two percentage points.

The question this module opens

A variable that hardly varies looks like a dead end. What else moves with it?

Put the range on the board and let it sit. Nine point seven to eleven point six. If you had asked the room to guess first, half of them would have said some county runs at twenty-five percent and another at three.

The instinct when a variable does not vary is to conclude there is nothing here. That instinct is wrong often enough to be worth breaking today. A flat variable is not a boring variable — it is one whose signal is somewhere other than its own spread.

There is a real reason this one is flat, and a student will ask. These are modelled small-area estimates, built by borrowing strength across areas, and that smooths county differences by construction. Part of the flatness is asthma; part of it is the estimator.

Do not answer the question yet. The last section of today answers it with a number, and the figures in between are how the room gets there. Say also, once, that this course describes and does not infer — no p-values, no tests — and that it will matter at the end.

matplotlib: the base layer

Every matplotlib figure is two objects

1. The figure is the canvas: its size, its resolution, and the file it saves to

2. The axes is one set of coordinates drawn on that canvas: the data, labels, and ticks

plt.subplots() returns both, and nearly every figure in practice begins with that call.

ax is not “axis”

An axes is an entire plotting area. An axis is one of its edges, reached as ax.xaxis or ax.yaxis. The naming is unfortunate and permanent.

Draw this on the board once and it stops being confusing for the rest of the course. The figure is a sheet of paper. The axes is one chart drawn on that sheet. A sheet can hold four charts, which is what multi-panel figures are, and Module 6 builds them.

The naming collision is genuinely bad. Axes-singular is the whole plot; axis is an edge. Everyone trips on it, including people who have written matplotlib for a decade.

Two styles exist in the wild: plt.plot(), which draws on whatever axes is current, and ax.plot(), which draws on the axes named. This course uses ax. for everything, because it is the style that survives a second panel and the one where the code says where the thing is going.

Histograms

A graphical summary of the distribution of one numeric variable. bins sets the number of bins, or their exact edges.

The default is a guess by the library, not a finding. Ten bins, with edges wherever the minimum and maximum happen to put them — here 9.89, 10.08, 10.27, numbers nobody would choose. The right panel sets the edges on round numbers with np.linspace, which is the Python version of R’s seq() inside breaks.

Read the shape, because it is the cold open restated. Everything piles up between ten and eleven and a half. Nothing is out in a tail.

Bin choice is a real decision and worth thirty seconds of live editing. Change 12 to 4 and the structure disappears into a block; change it to 40 and every county gets its own spike.

Contrast with a bar chart now, so it sticks later: a histogram’s x-axis is a number line, the order is fixed by arithmetic, and the bars touch because the bins are adjacent intervals.

Histograms + reference lines

A second layer drawn on the same axes. ax.axvline() adds a vertical line at a value.

Every call to ax. adds a layer to the same axes, in the order the lines run. That is the whole idea of layering, and it is the same idea the R half uses when it adds a density curve on top of a histogram. Module 6 does the density curve properly with seaborn.

The two numbers are 10.66 and 10.60. Mean and median sitting this close says the distribution is roughly symmetric, with no tail dragging the mean. When those two lines separate on a figure, the mean is being pulled and the median is the more honest summary.

label= on each layer is what ax.legend() reads. No label, no legend entry. That is the matplotlib habit to build: name the layer when you draw it.

Boxplots

A graphical summary of a distribution: the median, the interquartile range (the box), and the spread beyond it (the whiskers).

The line is the median, the box holds the middle half of the counties, the whiskers reach the furthest points within one and a half box-lengths, and anything beyond that is drawn as a single point. Four numbers and a picture in one glance. Make sure that is understood rather than nodded at, because Module 6 leans on it.

The grouped version is where matplotlib shows its age. In R the formula bwt ~ smoke does the split; here the split is a list comprehension, one Series per group, and the labels go on by hand. That clumsiness is the honest motivation for seaborn, which does the split from a column name. Do not apologise for it — show it once, then promise the shortcut.

Read the right panel: the boxes overlap almost entirely. Nonmetro sits a touch higher, 10.7 against 10.5 at the median. Metro status barely separates asthma. Keep that in mind for the end.

Pairs plots

pd.plotting.scatter_matrix() draws every pairwise scatter among several numeric columns, with a histogram of each column on the diagonal.

Most useful for a handful of numeric columns.

This is R’s pairs(). Nine panels, and the useful ones are the three below the diagonal — the three above are the same scatters with the axes swapped.

Read the bottom-left region with the room. Asthma against smoking rises clearly. Asthma against obesity rises, more loosely. Smoking against obesity rises too. Three variables, all moving together to different degrees, which is exactly the situation where reading one scatter alone can mislead.

The rename is not cosmetic. The panel edges are tiny, and smoking_prev_pct printed down the side of a small panel is unreadable. Short names for a figure, long names in the data — that split is worth adopting.

Pairs plots get unreadable past five or six columns. They are a first look, not a figure for a report.

seaborn and the grammar of graphics

The grammar of graphics and seaborn

A statistical figure is assembled from independent components (Wilkinson, 2005):

1. Data — a table, one row per observation

2. Mappings — which column drives which visual property: position, colour, shape, size

3. A mark — the geometric object that represents each row: a point, a line, a bar

In seaborn, the function name is the mark (scatterplot, lineplot, countplot), data= names the table, and every mapping is a keyword that takes a column name.

sns.scatterplot(data=joined,                # data
                x="smoking_prev_pct",       # mapping: column -> horizontal position
                y="asthma_prev_pct",        # mapping: column -> vertical position
                hue="metro")                # mapping: column -> colour

This is the idea the R half calls the grammar of graphics, and it is the same idea here, packaged differently. In ggplot2 the mark is a separate layer you add with + geom_point(). In seaborn the mark is fused into the function name. The data and the mappings are the same concepts with the same names.

The difference from matplotlib is the one to underline. matplotlib took columns — asthma["smoking_prev_pct"]. seaborn takes a DataFrame and column names, as strings, and does the lookup itself. That one change is why grouping becomes a single argument rather than a list comprehension.

This block is plain code, not a live cell; it is the anatomy, and the next slides run it.

First: an ordered categorical column

pandas sorts text alphabetically. When categories have a natural order, pd.Categorical() fixes it, and every figure and table then follows that order.

This is the Python version of R’s factor() with levels. The R half converts a 0/1 smoking code into a labelled factor; this file already has labels, so the problem here is order.

Read the first printout. Alphabetical puts Large central metro, Large fringe metro, Medium metro, Micropolitan, Noncore, Small metro — Small metro has drifted to the end, after the rural codes. Any figure drawn from that order would run from city to countryside and then jump back to a small city. The second printout runs one to six, most urban to most rural, which is the order the NCHS scheme intends.

Once the column is categorical, seaborn’s axes and legends follow the stored order without being told. The counting slide near the end of this section uses exactly this.

Text ↔︎ Plot (3 of 5)

… Represent each county with a point. Map metro status to the colour and the shape of each point.

hue= maps a column to colour; style= maps a column to marker shape. seaborn builds the legend.

Two keywords, and the figure now carries a third variable. Both name the same column, so colour and shape say the same thing twice — which is deliberate, because a figure printed in greyscale still separates the groups by shape.

The legend appeared on its own. That is the payoff of mappings: seaborn knows which column drove which property, so it can explain the mapping without being asked.

The shapes are seaborn’s defaults, a circle and a cross. The colours are defaults too, and the last build-up step replaces them. Read the cloud quickly: the nonmetro crosses sit further right. Rural counties smoke more. Park that.

… Map metro status to the colour and shape of each point. Title the figure and label both axes with units.

ax.set() sets several properties of the axes in one call.

The minimum for any figure that leaves a notebook

An x-label with units, a y-label with units, and a title that states what is plotted.

This is the step people skip, and it is a matplotlib call, not a seaborn one. seaborn drew on the axes; matplotlib labels it. That division of labour is permanent.

The callout is the rule worth remembering. An exploratory figure can stay unlabelled while it lives in a notebook only you read. The moment it goes into a slide, a report, or an email, you are no longer the reader, and smoking_prev_pct means nothing to someone who did not write the merge.

ax.set() is shorthand for ax.set_title(), ax.set_xlabel(), ax.set_ylabel(), all at once. Either works.

Two changes. The palette is a dictionary, group name to colour, which is worth preferring over a list: with a list the colours attach in whatever order seaborn meets the groups, and a filtered table can silently swap them between two figures in the same report. seaborn calls

data is seaborn’s first argument and its name can be omitted. pandas .plot.scatter() draws the same mark from the DataFrame itself.

The mappings x= and y= must always be named.

Three spellings, one figure. The R half shows the same thing: ggplot(data = birthwt, ...), ggplot(birthwt, ...), and the pipe.

The pandas version is the fastest route during exploration, especially straight after a groupby — .plot.bar() on a grouped Series is one line. It hands back a matplotlib axes, so every ax.set() call still works on it. Nothing learned is replaced.

The one rule: data can go unnamed, the mappings cannot. sns.scatterplot(asthma, "smoking_prev_pct", ...) raises a TypeError, because seaborn requires every mapping by name. That is a good rule; a figure’s code should say which column goes where.

Mapping versus setting

Mapping Setting
Drives the property from a column in the data a constant
Passed as a column name, to x, y, hue, style, size a value, to color, marker, s, alpha, linewidth, linestyle
Example hue="metro" color="#003594"
Legend built automatically none

Mappings are seaborn arguments. Settings are matplotlib arguments, which seaborn passes through to every mark.

This is the single most useful distinction in the module, and the R half draws it the same way: inside aes() is mapping, outside is setting.

The test to give the room: does the property change from one county to the next because of the data? Then it is a mapping, and it takes a column name. Is it the same for every point? Then it is a setting, and it takes a value.

The pairs of names matter. hue maps; color sets. style maps; marker sets. size maps; s sets. The mapping names are seaborn’s own, and the setting names are matplotlib’s, which is why they do not match. Leave the table up for a moment; the next four slides are examples of it.

Colour and shape

Both mapped to columns: colour to region, shape to metro status.

Two different columns now, so colour and shape carry two different variables. The legend grows two sections, one per mapping.

This is about as much as one scatter should carry. Six colours and two shapes is twelve combinations, and the eye cannot hold that. Module 6 shows the alternative, which is to split the groups into separate panels instead of separate colours.

The legend call moves it outside the axes because six regions inside the plotting area cover the points. bbox_to_anchor=(1.01, 1) means just past the right edge, at the top.

Size

Mapped to a column (left), and set to a constant (right).

The left panel is a bubble chart. Philadelphia and Allegheny are the two huge circles; Cameron, the smallest county at about 4,400 people, is a speck. sizes= sets the range of marker areas the mapping uses.

There is content in the left panel worth one sentence. The three highest-smoking counties — Forest at 21.8%, Sullivan, and Cameron — are the three smallest counties in the state, the specks at the far right. That is a preview of a problem Module 6 makes central: small counties produce the extreme values.

The right panel is a setting. s=80 makes every point the same size, no legend, nothing in the data involved.

Transparency

Set to a constant between 0 and 1. seaborn has no mapping for transparency.

Passing a column name to a setting is an error:

sns.scatterplot(data=asthma, x="smoking_prev_pct", y="asthma_prev_pct",  # data and positions
                alpha="metro")      # TypeError: alpha must be numeric or None, not a string

Large markers at forty percent opacity, so overlapping counties show up as darker patches. That is what transparency is for: seeing density where points pile up.

The R half maps alpha to a variable; seaborn simply does not offer that. The error in the code block is real, verified against the course’s seaborn version, and it is the clearest demonstration of mapping versus setting you will get. alpha is a setting. Give it a string and matplotlib tries to treat "metro" as a number.

If someone asks how to fade one group: draw two scatterplot calls, one per group, each with its own alpha. That is layering, and Module 6 does it properly.

Linetype

sns.lineplot() maps style= to the dash pattern of each line.

style= on a line chart does what it did on a scatter, for lines: one dash pattern per group. linewidth=2 is a setting, the same for both lines. To force one fixed pattern on every line, the setting is linestyle="--".

The filter on the fourth line is not decoration. This file badly undercounts 2020 — one death statewide for March to June 2020, when the real toll was in the thousands. From mid-2021 it is reliable. Say that out loud: a file can be real, official, and still wrong for some of its rows.

Read the figure. Nonmetro counties peak first and higher: 44.2 per 100,000 in December 2021, against 32.9 for metro counties in January 2022. Rates, not counts, so this compares places of very different size fairly. Module 6 makes the rate-versus-count question its centrepiece.

pd.to_datetime matters here: without it the months are text, and the axis would space them evenly in alphabetical order.

Default aesthetics

Once a column is mapped, seaborn chooses the rest:

1. Axis labels — the column names: smoking_prev_pct

2. Legend title — the column name: metro

3. Colours — the deep palette, assigned in the order the groups first appear in the data

4. Markers and dashes — a fixed sequence: circle, cross, diamond, …

5. Category order — order of appearance, unless the column is a pd.Categorical

Each default has an override: ax.set(), .set_title() on the legend, palette=, markers=, order= or a categorical column.

This is the list of everything the build-up replaced, stated once in general form. The R half makes the same point about ggplot2: once a mapping exists, scales and legends follow, and every one of them can be overridden.

The defaults are guesses made by someone who never saw this figure. They are a good starting point for exploration and nearly never right for a report. The habit worth building is to read each one on a draft figure and ask whether it says what you mean.

Number five connects back to the categorical slide. If urbanicity is plain text, seaborn orders it by first appearance in the file, which is whatever order the rows happen to be in.

A truncated axis manufactures a difference

Identical data. The right panel implies Philadelphia has three times the asthma burden of Clinton County. The true difference is 0.4 percentage points.

The rule for bars

A bar encodes value by length, so its baseline must be zero. Truncating the axis breaks the correspondence between length and value.

Ask the room what the left panel shows first. Someone will say Philadelphia is the worst; someone sharper will notice the bars are all nearly the same height. That second observation is the real content.

Then run the right panel and stop talking for a moment. One call to set_ylim and the story changes entirely. Nobody has to be dishonest to produce it — the usual route is an analyst who zoomed in to see the differences and never zoomed back out.

The rule is specifically about bars. Bars encode by length, so the baseline must be zero. A line or a scatter encodes by position, and those can start anywhere; the scatters today do not start at zero and are not misleading.

If someone asks whether the left panel wastes space: yes, and it is worth it. The empty space is the honest statement that these counties are nearly the same.

Describing what the figure shows

.describe() summarises every numeric column at once

Eight numbers per column: the count, the mean, the standard deviation, the minimum, the three quartiles, and the maximum.

count is the first number to read

It reports non-missing values. A count below the number of rows means missing data is present in that column.

Read the table across rather than down, because the comparison is the finding. Asthma has a standard deviation of 0.38. Smoking has 2.32 — six times as wide. Obesity has 2.26.

That single comparison is the cold open expressed as a number: asthma does not vary much across Pennsylvania counties, while the things that might explain it vary a great deal.

The callout is the habit worth installing. count first, every time. Here all three read 67: CDC PLACES models an estimate for every county, so there is no suppression in this file — unlike the births file from Module 4.

Mean 10.66 against median 10.60 — the same closeness the histogram’s two lines showed. Roughly symmetric, no tail.

.groupby() and .agg() describe each group

The comparison this table makes

Metro and nonmetro counties differ by 0.17 percentage points in asthma prevalence, and by 2.34 percentage points in smoking prevalence.

This is the boxplot from earlier as a table, and the numbers say what the overlapping boxes implied. Asthma: 10.58 metro against 10.75 nonmetro. Smoking: 16.08 against 18.42.

The asthma difference is a sixth of a percentage point and means very little. The smoking difference is more than two full points. Same table, and one column is carrying nearly all of the signal — which is what the scatter in the build-up showed with the nonmetro markers sitting to the right.

Always print the group sizes. A difference in means over four counties and one over thirty-seven deserve very different confidence, and without the count nobody can tell which they are reading.

.corr() measures how two columns move together

A correlation runs from −1 to +1. It reports the strength and direction of a straight-line relationship, and nothing else.

The answer to the opening question: asthma moves with smoking, at r = +0.659 across 67 counties.

What a correlation cannot establish

It cannot establish cause. It cannot detect a curved relationship. It is distorted by outliers. And these are county averages, so it describes counties, not people.

This is the payoff of the cold open, so slow down. Asthma barely moves between Pennsylvania counties — under two points across all sixty-seven. Smoking moves across eleven. And they move together at plus 0.659, the strongest relationship in the course. Obesity comes in at 0.476: real, weaker, and worth saying so the room does not conclude that everything correlates with everything.

Now the caveat that carries the most weight. These are county averages. A correlation across counties describes counties. It does not establish that the people who smoke are the people with asthma — inferring the second from the first is the ecological fallacy, and they will meet the name formally elsewhere. Today they meet the caution.

It is consistent with smoking causing asthma, with both sharing upstream causes — income, occupation, housing — and with combinations of those. The figure earns the relationship. It does not earn the mechanism. No p-value here, by design.

Module 6 opens by showing how a single point can move this number, which is why the scatter always comes before the correlation.
