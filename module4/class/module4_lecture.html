Twenty-three blanks.

Sometimes official statistics aren’t reported because they’re not trustworthy!

In twenty-three Pennsylvania counties, no infant mortality rate is published

The figure is not secret. It is unstable — too few births, too few deaths, and a rate computed from a handful of events moves wildly on one additional case.

The question this session opens

Which twenty-three counties, and what do they have in common?

This session establishes the DataFrame: a table whose columns keep their names, whose rows stay aligned through every operation, and which can be filtered, grouped, and joined to a second table.

Required from Module 3: vectorised arithmetic, boolean masks, axis=, and np.nan.

Read the number and stop for a second. Twenty-three of sixty-seven. More than a third of the state has no published infant mortality rate.

The instinct in the room will be that somebody is hiding something, and that instinct is worth naming and then dismantling. Nobody is hiding anything. A county with four hundred births a year and two infant deaths has a rate of 5.0 per 1,000; one more death makes it 7.5. The rate did not change because health changed, it changed because one family had the worst year of their lives. Publishing that number invites a reader to compare it to Allegheny’s, and the comparison is meaningless.

So the withholding is a statistical judgment, not an administrative one. That framing carries the whole module and it is the thing to keep coming back to.

Do not answer which counties yet. The payoff slide has it, and the answer is more interesting than the list — the counties have something in common, and finding out what requires joining this table to a second one. That join is the reason pandas exists.

Module 3 ended with two arrays held in alignment by nothing but care. Today that stops being the student’s problem.

A DataFrame is a table with named columns

pd.read_csv() reads an entire table in one call. The columns keep their names, and every column keeps its own type.

import pandas as pd is the universal convention

As with np, the alias is not required by the language, and departing from it makes code harder for others to read.

Contrast this directly with Module 3 and be explicit about it. Reading two columns there took two genfromtxt calls, and keeping them aligned was a promise nothing enforced. Here one call reads eight columns, and the alignment is structural — it is what a DataFrame is.

.shape returns (67, 8), the same attribute name as NumPy, and that is deliberate: a DataFrame is built on arrays. Rows first, columns second.

Say the source out loud: County Health Rankings 2024, all 67 Pennsylvania counties. Every number in this module is real and every one of them describes somewhere a student in the room is from.

Four methods describe a table before any analysis

1. .head(n) shows the first rows; .tail(n) the last

2. .shape gives rows and columns

3. .dtypes gives the type of each column

4. .info() combines all three and adds the count of non-missing values

Inspect before computing

A column read as text when it should be numeric produces no error at read time. The failure appears later, when arithmetic on it returns something wrong or raises TypeError.

This is the habit to install today: read the file, then look at it, before doing anything else. Four keystrokes. It catches the class of bug that otherwise surfaces forty lines later when the numbers look strange and nobody knows why.

object in the dtype output means text, and it is pandas’ least helpful piece of naming. When a column that should be numeric shows up as object, something in it is not a number — a stray comma, a footnote marker, the word “suppressed.” That is a real diagnostic and it comes back on the cleaning slide.

.info() is the one to reach for in practice because the non-null count is where missing data first announces itself. Run it live on this file and let them see 44 where the others say 67.

.info() reports where values are missing

Every column has 67 entries. infant_mortality_per_1000 has 44.

The gap is the finding, not an obstacle

Twenty-three counties are missing a rate. That count is the answer to the question this session opened, and it appears here in the second minute of inspection.

This is the moment to slow down. The cold open asked which twenty-three counties, and .info() just found the twenty-three without a single line of analysis. Point at the 44 and let them do the subtraction.

Two other columns have gaps worth naming: teen_births_per_1000 is missing one, and child_mortality_per_100k is missing seven. Same reason, same suppression rule, different thresholds because the underlying counts differ.

The general lesson is that .info() is a missingness report and most people skip past it looking for the dtypes. Reading the non-null column first is a better habit.

.describe() summarises every numeric column

count is the number of present values, so it differs by column wherever data is missing.

count is the first row for a reason

Every other statistic in the table is computed over that count. A mean of 6.15 describes 44 counties, not 67, and the number of counties it describes belongs in any sentence that reports it.

The count row is the one nobody reads and it is the most important one on the slide. This continues Module 3’s rule about reporting the denominator, now with pandas printing the denominator unprompted.

The numbers, if useful: infant mortality across the 44 reporting counties runs 3.4 to 10.2, mean 6.15, median 6.0. Child poverty across all 67 runs from single digits to 28.3% in Philadelphia.

.round(2) is there because the raw output carries six decimal places of false precision. Formatting is graded in this course, so model it.

A single column is a Series

A Series is one column: an array of values plus an index that labels them. A DataFrame is a set of Series sharing one index.

.count() and .size answer different questions

.size is the number of rows. .count() is the number of rows with a value. Their difference is the number missing.

The index is the piece that makes a Series more than an array, and it is what keeps rows aligned through a sort or a filter. Module 3’s parallel arrays had no index, which is exactly why sorting one and not the other broke them silently.

Here the index is just 0 through 66, so it looks like decoration. It stops looking like decoration on the .groupby() slides, where the index becomes the region name.

.mean() on a Series skips missing values by default — the opposite of NumPy’s .mean(), which propagates nan and returns nan. That difference is worth stating plainly because it bites people who learned NumPy first, which is everyone in this room. pandas assumes skipping is what was wanted. It is a convenience with a cost, and the cost is that the denominator changes without saying so.

.loc selects by label and .iloc by position

.loc[row_label, column_label] uses names. .iloc[row_number, column_number] uses positions, following Module 3’s zero-indexing.

.loc includes its endpoint; .iloc excludes it

.loc["Adams":"Beaver"] returns Beaver. .iloc[0:3] stops at position 2. Slicing by label and slicing by position follow different rules, and this is a documented inconsistency in pandas rather than a mistake in the reading.

.set_index("county") is what makes .loc worth using here. Without it the row labels are 0 through 66 and .loc and .iloc do nearly the same thing, which teaches nothing.

The endpoint inconsistency is genuinely confusing and the honest framing is that label slicing includes the endpoint because a reader asking for Adams through Beaver means to include Beaver, while position slicing follows Python’s universal exclusive rule from Module 1. Both are defensible; together they are a trap.

Allegheny is 6.2 per 1,000. Centre is 4.6 with child poverty at 9.9%. Those two counties have appeared in every module since Module 1 and they will appear again on the correlation slide.

If someone asks whether births["Allegheny"] works: it does not. Square brackets on a DataFrame select columns, and asking for a row that way raises KeyError. That is the next slide’s error.

KeyError names a label that is not there

KeyError: 'infant_mortality' — square brackets on a DataFrame select a column by name, and no column carries that name. The actual column is infant_mortality_per_1000.

KeyError is a spelling error nine times in ten

The interpreter reports the label it was given, not the label that was meant. Resolution: print df.columns.tolist() and copy the name from the output.

Run this one broken on the projector. It is thirty seconds and the traceback is ugly enough to be memorable.

The reason it earns a slide is that KeyError is the single most common pandas error and the message looks more alarming than it is. Once they know it means “that name is not in this table,” it stops costing them time.

The units suffix in these column names is deliberate on the data side and it is what makes the error likely here. infant_mortality_per_1000 is unambiguous about what the number means, and the cost of that clarity is more typing. That trade is the right one — a column called rate in a file you open in six months is worthless.

Tell them to copy column names rather than type them. df.columns.tolist() exists for this.

A boolean mask filters rows

The mask is Module 3’s boolean array. Passing it to [] keeps the rows where it is True, and every column comes along.

This is what the DataFrame buys

Module 3’s mask filtered one array and left the names behind. Here the county names, the rates, and the incomes all move together, because they are rows of one object.

Eleven counties have child poverty above 20%. Philadelphia is the highest at 28.3%.

Make the Module 3 comparison explicit, because this is the payoff for the complaint that module ended on. There, filtering pm25 gave 22 numbers and no way to know which counties they were without applying the same mask to a second array and trusting the alignment. Here the answer arrives labelled.

The mask itself is worth printing once. births["children_in_poverty_pct"] > 20 is a Series of 67 True/False values with the same index, and it is the identical construct from last week wearing a label.

Combining conditions requires &, |, and parentheses

The parentheses are mandatory

& binds more tightly than >. Without parentheses, Python evaluates 20 & births[...] first and raises TypeError. This is the most common pandas error after KeyError.

Resolution: parenthesise every comparison. (a > 1) & (b < 2), never a > 1 & b < 2.

This is a check-in. Put the unparenthesised version on the screen, ask what it does, and run it. The error is loud, which is a mercy, but the message mentions ambiguous truth values and points at nothing useful.

Four counties clear both thresholds: Fayette, Mifflin, Philadelphia, and Venango. Mifflin has the highest infant mortality in the state at 10.2 per 1,000 with child poverty at 20.6%.

This is the same &-not-and rule from Module 3, and it is on the spec twice because the parentheses make it harder here than it was there. Last week the operands were bare array names. Here they are bracketed column selections, so the expression is already busy and the missing parentheses are harder to see.

Handle the substance carefully. Four counties appearing in both lists is a structural observation about where poverty and infant death occur together, and it is not a statement about any family. Keep it at the county level and keep it structural — §5 is explicit about this and the room will follow the framing that gets modelled.

.isin() matches against a list of values

.isin() replaces a chain of == comparisons joined by |.

These four counties have run through the whole course. Schuylkill and Centre are the Module 1 pair — 41.7 against 4.4 overdose deaths per 100,000 at almost identical population — and they turn up here at 7.5 and 4.6 infant deaths per 1,000. The same two counties, a different outcome, the same direction.

Point that out and then stop, because a four-county comparison is an anecdote. The correlation slide is where it becomes evidence.

Written without .isin() this filter is four == comparisons and three pipes, all inside parentheses. Show that once so the shortcut looks like a shortcut.

.assign() adds a derived column

A rate is deaths divided by population, times 100,000. The arithmetic is vectorised — one expression over every row.

.assign() returns a new DataFrame

The original is unchanged. births["new"] = ... modifies in place; .assign() does not, which is what makes it safe to chain.

Recomputing a column the file already contains looks pointless and it is the opposite. This is a data check: rate_check should match rate_per_100k to rounding, and if it does not, either the population column is the wrong year or the published rate uses a different denominator. Do this to any file before trusting it.

They match here, so the file is internally consistent. Say so — a check that passes is still a check.

The lambda is the part to explain slowly. Inside .assign(), lambda d: means “the DataFrame as it exists at this point in the chain,” which matters because the previous step filtered it to 2023. Writing overdose["overdose_deaths"] instead would reach back to the unfiltered table and the lengths would not match.

The underscore in 100_000 is Python’s digit separator. Purely for reading; the interpreter ignores it.

.sort_values() orders rows

ascending=False sorts high to low. Missing values sort to the end regardless of direction.

Sorting moves whole rows

Every column travels with its row. This is the guarantee Module 3’s parallel arrays could not make.

Mifflin 10.2, Huntingdon 9.1, Venango 8.9, Crawford 8.8, Clearfield 8.1. All five are nonmetro, and that is the first quiet hint of where this module is going.

The point on the slide is the one to stress. Sorting a NumPy array of rates and forgetting to sort the names alongside it produces a table that looks right and is wrong in every row. Here it cannot happen.

Worth asking: is this list the five worst counties in Pennsylvania for infant mortality? No. It is the five worst of the forty-four that report. Twenty-three counties are not in this ranking at all, and nothing about the sort announces that. Missing data does not raise its hand.

.groupby() splits, applies, and combines

.groupby(column) splits the rows into groups, applies the aggregation to each, and combines the results into a table indexed by the grouping value.

.agg() takes several functions at once

["count", "mean", "median"] produces three columns. count reports how many rows carried a value in each group, which is the denominator for the other two.

This is Module 3’s axis= generalized, and saying so connects the two modules. There the collapse ran along a fixed dimension of a rectangle. Here the groups are defined by the contents of a column, so there can be six of them or sixty-seven, and pandas works it out.

Read the count column across the years. It is not constant — the number of counties reporting changes year to year, because suppression depends on that year’s counts. The mean of a different set of counties each year is not a time series, and comparing 2019 to 2023 as if it were is a mistake the table invites.

Split-apply-combine is the name for this pattern and it is worth giving them the phrase. It is the same idea as R’s group_by() plus summarise(), which half of them have already seen in the other half of this course.

Grouping by a second table’s column requires a join

The overdose file has no region column. The county reference table does.

The grouping variables live in a different file

Neither region nor metro appears in any outcome file. Every grouped comparison in this module is possible only after a join, which is the honest reason .merge() is taught.

This is the motivating slide for the rest of the session, so do not rush it.

Six regions, unevenly sized: South Central and North Central have 14 counties each, Northeast has 8. Thirty-seven metro counties against thirty nonmetro, using the NCHS urban–rural classification rather than anything invented for this course.

.value_counts() is the categorical counterpart to .describe() and it is how a text column gets inspected. It sorts by frequency, which is usually what is wanted and occasionally surprising.

The design point worth stating: this separation is good practice, not an inconvenience. The county attributes live in one file, each outcome lives in its own, and they are joined on demand. Storing region alongside every outcome in every file means updating it in six places when a definition changes.

.merge() joins two tables on a shared key

on= names the key column. The result carries the columns of both tables for every row whose key matched.

Eight columns plus seven columns, minus the shared key, gives fourteen. Sixty-seven rows in, sixty-seven rows out, which means every county matched.

That clean result is the thing to be suspicious of, and the next two slides are about why.

The word for the shared column is the key. It has to identify a row uniquely in at least one of the tables, and here county does that in both — sixty-seven distinct names on each side.

For the R half: this is left_join() and friends, same concept, same four join types, different spelling.

The join type decides what happens to unmatched rows

1. inner keeps only keys present in both — the default

2. left keeps every row of the left table, filling unmatched columns with NaN

3. outer keeps every row of both

An inner join deletes rows silently

Rows whose key fails to match are dropped with no warning. Compare the row count before and after every join. A join that loses rows has either found a real gap or a spelling problem.

All three give 67 here because the keys agree perfectly, which makes this a poor demonstration of the difference and an excellent demonstration of the check. The habit is what matters: print the shape before, print it after, and know why any difference exists.

The default being inner is the dangerous part. A student who never passes how= is silently dropping unmatched rows in every join they will ever write, and in a real analysis that is how counties disappear.

left is the right default in practice for this kind of work: keep every row of the analysis table, attach what reference data exists, and let the NaNs show where it did not. Recommend it.

The next slide is where the keys stop agreeing.

A key that differs by one character does not match

“Mc Kean” and “McKean” are different strings. The row survives a left join with NaN in every borrowed column, and an inner join deletes it outright.

NaN in a joined column is a failed match, not missing data

The population of McKean County is published and known. It is absent here because the key did not match, and the two causes require different fixes.

This is a real spelling mismatch between County Health Rankings and the Census, not an invented example. The version of the file in data/ has already been cleaned, which is why the earlier joins were tidy; this slide puts the original spelling back so the failure is visible.

The failure mode is the lesson. Potter and Tioga match, McKean does not, and the output looks almost entirely correct. One NaN in a table of three is easy to see. One in a table of sixty-seven is not, and nothing raises.

The fix is .str.replace(" ", "") on both sides before joining, or a lookup table of known variants. What matters more than the fix is the check that finds it: after any join, count the NaNs in a column that came from the right-hand table. Any count above zero is either a real gap or a broken key, and it has to be one or the other.

Other keys that break the same way: county names with and without the word “County”, FIPS codes read as integers on one side and strings with leading zeros on the other, and dates as text against dates as dates. Every one of those has cost somebody a day.

.isna() counts what is missing

.isna() returns a table of booleans; .sum() counts the True values in each column, because True equals 1.

df.isna().sum() is the standard missingness report

One line, every column, run immediately after every read and every join.

Twenty-three, seven, and one. Infant mortality, child mortality, teen births — three columns, three different suppression thresholds, all applied to the same 67 counties.

Why they differ: the threshold is on the underlying count, not on the county. Infant deaths are rarer than child deaths, so more counties fall below the cutoff for the infant measure. That is worth a sentence because it explains why missingness is not a property of a county.

The chain is worth reading aloud in pieces. .isna() gives a 67-by-8 grid of True and False. .sum() on a DataFrame collapses down the rows by default and returns one number per column. Same True-is-1 arithmetic as Module 3’s mask counting.

Missing is not zero

Filling with zero asserts that no infant died in those twenty-three counties. The state withheld the figure precisely because it is not zero.

.fillna() writes a claim into the data

Every fill value is an assertion about the world. Zero asserts absence. The mean asserts the county is typical. Both may be defensible; neither is neutral, and both belong in the writeup.

6.15 against 4.04. A third of the state filled with a false zero drags the state figure down by more than two points, and the resulting number is not wrong in a subtle way — it is a claim that twenty-three counties had no infant deaths.

The instinct to fill with zero is nearly universal and it comes from spreadsheets, where a blank cell and a zero look similar and behave similarly. Name that and correct it once, firmly. This is the third module in a row where the same rule has appeared: Module 2’s .get(key, 0), Module 3’s np.nanmean(), and now this. It is the most important non-syntactic idea in the course.

.dropna() is the honest default and it is what pandas does silently in .mean(). The subtlety is that dropping is also a decision — it produces a mean over the counties that reported, which is a different population than the one asked about, and the count is what makes that visible.

There is no fill value that is right here. The right move is to report the 44 and say which counties are absent.

Grouped rates are biased downward by suppression

.sum() skips NaN, but the denominator counts every county

A suppressed county contributes its population and none of its deaths. The rate is therefore too low, and no error is raised.

This is the most important slide in the module and it is a silent error, so give it the time.

Look at where the bias lands. Southwest and Southeast are unaffected — 42.9 and 41.3 either way — because no county in them is suppressed. Northwest is off by 2.0 and North Central by 1.8. The bias is largest in rural regions, which is exactly where suppression concentrates, and exactly where an underestimate does the most harm.

Say what that means outside the code. A published regional rate computed the naive way makes rural Pennsylvania look better than it is, in a dataset used to allocate resources. Nobody had to be careless for that to happen. .sum() skipping NaN is a reasonable default that becomes a systematic error the moment it appears in a ratio.

The fix is on the slide: drop the suppressed rows from the numerator and the denominator, so the rate describes the counties that actually reported. Then say how many were dropped.

The general rule, and write it down: whenever a ratio is computed over a group, confirm that the numerator and the denominator cover the same rows. That sentence catches this whole class of bug.

pd.crosstab() counts one categorical against another

The pattern in the table

Five of thirty-seven metro counties are suppressed. Eighteen of thirty nonmetro counties are. Suppression is not distributed at random across Pennsylvania.

Here is the payoff assembling itself. The cold open asked what the twenty-three counties have in common, and this two-by-two answers it: they are rural.

Work the proportions out loud, because the raw counts understate it. Thirteen and a half percent of metro counties are suppressed against sixty percent of nonmetro ones. Two out of three rural Pennsylvania counties have no published infant mortality rate.

Note what had to happen to see this. The suppression flag came from the outcome file, the metro classification came from the reference file, and the two were joined a slide ago. Neither file can produce this table alone. That is the argument for .merge() in one sentence, and it is worth making it out loud.

pd.crosstab() takes two Series and returns counts. Adding normalize="index" gives row proportions, which is often the more readable form — mention it, and let the lab use it.

pd.to_datetime() makes a date column behave like a date

A text column sorts alphabetically. A datetime column sorts chronologically and exposes .dt.year, .dt.month, and .dt.day_name().

Forty-nine months, March 2020 through March 2024. The peak is January 2022 at 4,294 deaths statewide — the Omicron wave, and it is the largest single month in the file.

The yearly totals tell the shape of the pandemic in five lines: 975 in a partial 2020, then 10,875 and 11,109 in the two full years, then 3,647, then 916. Let that sit for a second without commentary. This is Module 2’s COVID story returning with a real date column underneath it.

Why to_datetime() matters beyond convenience: as text, "2020-03-01" sorts before "2020-12-01" correctly but "3/1/2020" does not, and nothing warns. ISO format hides the problem. Converting makes the ordering a property of the type rather than a property of the formatting.

.dt is the accessor for datetime methods, the same way .str is the accessor for text methods on the next slide. Both exist because a Series holds many values and the method has to apply to all of them.

.str applies string methods to a whole column

Module 1’s string methods, applied through .str to every value in the column at once.

A numeric column read as text is the most common import defect

A thousands separator is enough to make pandas read a whole column as object. Arithmetic on it either raises TypeError or, worse, concatenates.

Every defect on this slide is one that turns up in real public health files: leading whitespace from a spreadsheet export, inconsistent capitalisation, the McKean spelling, and comma-separated thousands.

The deaths column is the one to demonstrate. Before .astype(int) it is text, and "712" + "8" gives "7128" rather than 720. That is a silent wrong answer, not an error, which makes it more dangerous than the TypeError. Run it if there is time.

.str.title() is a rough tool and it is worth being honest about its limits. It fixes “PHILADELPHIA” cleanly, and here it happens to leave “Mc Kean” as “Mc Kean” — capitalising each word — so the space removal that follows is what actually repairs the key. Change the input slightly and it misbehaves: "MCKEAN" comes back as "Mckean", because title() capitalises after a space and nowhere else. Automated cleaning gets most of the way and the residue needs a human. Do not promise them a one-liner that always works.

Chaining reads left to right: strip the whitespace, fix the case, remove the internal space. Each step returns a Series, so the next method attaches to it.

.rename() and .drop() tidy the columns

.rename(columns={old: new}) renames by mapping. .drop(columns=[...]) removes columns. Both return a new DataFrame and leave the original unchanged.

.drop() needs columns= or index=

.drop("county") alone raises KeyError, because .drop() defaults to removing a row with that label. Naming the axis explicitly avoids the ambiguity.

Eight columns become six. Nothing was computed here; this is housekeeping, and it earns a slide because unnamed and unwanted columns are what make a printed table unreadable.

The judgment call worth flagging: renaming away the units is a real loss. infant_mortality_per_1000 says what the number is, and infant_mortality does not. Shorten a name for typing convenience and something has to carry the units instead — a comment, a docstring, the report text. Trading clarity for brevity is a choice, not an improvement.

.drop() defaulting to rows surprises everyone, because dropping a column is the far more common operation. Always pass columns=.

The other thing to name: both methods return copies, which is why the chain works and why births still has eight columns afterwards. There is an inplace=True argument on most pandas methods and it is going away — do not teach it, and tell them to ignore it when they find it in older answers online.

.melt() turns wide data into long

Wide: one row per county, one column per year. Long: one row per county-year, with the year as data.

Long form is what .groupby() and plotting expect

.pivot() reverses the operation. Published tables arrive wide because wide is readable; analysis wants long because long is groupable.

Three rows become nine. id_vars names the columns that stay as identifiers, and everything else gets folded into a name column and a value column.

The reason this matters: grouping by year is impossible in the wide table, because the year is not a variable there — it is spread across three column names. In the long table the year is a column of data and .groupby("year") works. Same numbers, different shape, and only one shape can answer the question.

The overdose file already arrives long, one row per county-year, which is why nothing so far has needed this. Files from an agency’s published tables usually arrive wide, and this is the first thing that happens to them.

Note the year values come out as strings, because they were column names. .astype(int) fixes it and that is a good reason to look at .dtypes after any reshape.

Chaining expresses a pipeline as one expression

Each method returns a DataFrame, so the next attaches to it. The outer parentheses allow the chain to break across lines.

Read a chain top to bottom as a sentence

Take 2023, attach the region, drop the suppressed counties, group by region, summarise, sort.

This is the module assembling into one object, and it is worth saying that everything on this slide was taught separately in the last thirty minutes.

counties_reporting is on the slide deliberately. It is the denominator, it is carried through the chain, and it is what makes the mean interpretable — the Northeast figure is over seven counties, North Central over eleven. Without that column the table invites a comparison it cannot support.

The named-aggregation syntax, name=("column", "function"), is the readable form and it is worth preferring over the dictionary form they will find online. The column name says what the number is.

Two cautions on chaining. It is harder to debug, because there is no intermediate variable to inspect — the fix is to comment out the tail of the chain and run the head. And past six or seven steps it stops being readable; break it into two named pieces at that point.

.query() is a shorthand for boolean filtering with the condition as a string. It avoids the parenthesis problem entirely, which is either a convenience or a way to never learn the parentheses, so it appears here after the rule and not before it.

Twenty-three of Pennsylvania’s sixty-seven counties have no published infant mortality rate. Eighteen of them are nonmetro.

Their median population is 37,607. The counties that do report have a median population of 157,825.

Why suppression is the honest answer

An infant mortality rate is deaths per 1,000 live births. A county with roughly 400 births a year and two infant deaths reports 5.0. One additional death moves it to 7.5 — a fifty percent increase driven by a single event.

Publishing that figure invites a comparison it cannot support. Withholding it states that the data cannot answer the question at this resolution.

Land it and stop.

Then make the second point, which is the one that outlasts the module. Suppression is not the absence of information. That two-thirds of rural Pennsylvania counties cannot support a stable infant mortality rate is itself a finding — it says the events are rare and the populations are small, and both of those are facts about rural Pennsylvania worth knowing.

Where this leaves an analyst. The rate cannot be published county by county, so the options are to aggregate counties into regions until the counts are stable, to pool several years, or to report the raw counts alongside an explicit statement that no rate is computable. All three are honest. Filling the blanks with zero is not, and neither is quietly analysing the forty-four and calling the result “Pennsylvania.”

Keep the framing structural. These are counties with small populations, long distances to obstetric care, and hospitals that have closed maternity wards. That is a statement about infrastructure. §5 is explicit that this story is never framed as individual choice, and the room takes its cue from how the instructor says it.

The correlation the data does support, if it comes up: child poverty against infant mortality is +0.646 across the counties that report, and median income against infant mortality is −0.602. Module 5 draws that scatter.
