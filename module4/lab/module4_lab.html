Two facts that recur throughout

A rate is deaths / population * 100000. A blank is suppression, not zero — a count too small to publish. Neither region nor metro appears in any outcome file, so every grouped comparison below requires a join.

Exercise 1 — Find the missing values

Exercise 1 — Find the missing values — your turn

Your turn. Count the missing values in every column of the births file, then pull out the count for infant mortality. import pandas as pd

births = pd.read_csv("data/pa_maternal_infant.csv")

missing_per_column = births.isna().sum()
n_suppressed = missing_per_column["infant_mortality_per_1000"]

print(missing_per_column)
print(f"\nCounties with no published infant mortality rate: {n_suppressed}")

Different columns, different thresholds

The suppression cutoff applies to the underlying count, not to the county. Infant deaths are rarer than child deaths, so more counties fall below the cutoff for the infant measure. Missingness is a property of the measure and the county together.

Exercise 2 — Combine two conditions

Worked example. & means and, | means or. Each comparison needs its own parentheses, because & binds more tightly than >.

Exercise 2 — Combine two conditions — your turn

Your turn. Find the counties where more than 6% of children are uninsured and more than 15% live in poverty. Read data/pa_maternal_infant.csv as births; the columns are uninsured_children_pct and children_in_poverty_pct. strained is the rows of births that meet both conditions, as a DataFrame with all of its columns. n_strained is how many rows it has. Print n_strained, then each county with its two percentages.

# The first blank is & and the second is >.
# Writing `and` instead of & raises ValueError: a comparison on 67 rows gives
# 67 truth values, and `and` demands exactly one.
# Dropping the parentheses raises TypeError, because & binds tighter than >.
# Seven counties meet both conditions.
import pandas as pd births = pd.read_csv("data/pa_maternal_infant.csv") strained = births[ (births["uninsured_children_pct"] > 6) & (births["children_in_poverty_pct"] > 15) ] n_strained = len(strained) print(f"Counties meeting both conditions: {n_strained}") print(strained[["county", "uninsured_children_pct", "children_in_poverty_pct"]].to_string(index=False))

Exercise 3 — Derive a column

Exercise 3 — Derive a column — your turn

Your turn. Compute the 2022 firearm death rate per 100,000 and confirm it reproduces the published column. Read data/pa_firearm_county_year.csv as firearm. f2022 is its 2022 rows with one new column, my_rate, added with .assign(): firearm_deaths / population * 100_000. allegheny is Allegheny’s row of f2022, kept as a one-row DataFrame (filter on county). Print its firearm_deaths, population, rate_per_100k, and my_rate. The last two agree to rounding.

# The blank is 100000 — a rate per 100,000 multiplies the proportion by
# 100,000. Python allows 100_000 with an underscore for readability.
# Allegheny recorded 187 firearm deaths in 2022, giving 15.27 per 100,000,
# which matches the published 15.3 to rounding.
# Recomputing a published column is a DATA CHECK: a mismatch means the
# population denominator or the published rate uses different assumptions.
import pandas as pd firearm = pd.read_csv("data/pa_firearm_county_year.csv") f2022 = firearm[firearm["year"] == 2022].assign( my_rate=lambda d: d["firearm_deaths"] / d["population"] * 100_000 ) allegheny = f2022[f2022["county"] == "Allegheny"] print(allegheny[["county", "firearm_deaths", "population", "rate_per_100k", "my_rate"]].round(2).to_string(index=False)) 
import pandas as pd

firearm = pd.read_csv("data/pa_firearm_county_year.csv")

f2022 = firearm[firearm["year"] == 2022].assign(

Exercise 5 — Group by a joined column — your turn

Your turn. Compare mean low birthweight across the six regions, and report how many counties each region contains. Read data/pa_maternal_infant.csv as births and data/pa_counties.csv as counties. joined is births left-joined to counties on county. by_region is a table with one row per region and two columns, size and mean: group joined on region, then aggregate low_birthweight_pct with .agg(["size", "mean"]) and round to 2 places. Print it sorted by mean, highest first.

# The blank is region, in quotes.
# The regions are unevenly sized — South Central and North Central hold 14
# counties each, Northeast only 8 — so a regional mean is an average over a
# different number of counties each time.
# Northwest is highest at 8.44%; North Central lowest at 6.94%.
import pandas as pd births = pd.read_csv("data/pa_maternal_infant.csv") counties = pd.read_csv("data/pa_counties.csv") joined = births.merge(counties, on="county", how="left") by_region = joined.groupby("region")["low_birthweight_pct"].agg(["size", "mean"]).round(2) print(by_region.sort_values("mean", ascending=False)) 
import pandas as pd

births = pd.read_csv("data/pa_maternal_infant.csv")
counties = pd.read_csv("data/pa_counties.csv")

joined = births.merge(counties, on="county", how="left")

by_region = joined.groupby("region")["low_birthweight_pct"].agg(["size", "mean"]).round(2)

print(by_region.sort_values("mean", ascending=False))

Exercise 6 — The suppression bias

Exercise 6 — The suppression bias

Worked example. A grouped rate divides summed deaths by summed population. .sum() skips NaN — but the population denominator still counts every county.

Exercise 6 — The suppression bias — your turn

Your turn. Measure how far suppression biases the 2023 Northwest overdose rate. A suppressed county has a blank death count, which .sum() skips, but its population still counts in the denominator.

Read data/pa_overdose_county_year.csv as overdose and data/pa_counties.csv as counties. o is the 2023 rows of overdose, left-joined to counties on county. nw is the rows of o whose region is "Northwest". Compute two rates, both per 100,000:

  • naive: the sum of overdose_deaths over all of nw, divided by the sum of population_2023 over all of nw.
  • correct: the same calculation over reported, which is nw with the rows missing overdose_deaths dropped.

Print both rates and the bias, naive - correct.

# The blank is dropna. `subset=` restricts the drop to rows missing that one
# column, rather than dropping any row with a gap anywhere.
# The naive figure is 37.5 and the correct one is 39.5, so the naive rate
# understates the Northwest by 2.0 per 100,000.
# The suppressed counties contributed population but no deaths.
import pandas as pd overdose = pd.read_csv("data/pa_overdose_county_year.csv") counties = pd.read_csv("data/pa_counties.csv") o = overdose[overdose["year"] == 2023].merge(counties, on="county", how="left") nw = o[o["region"] == "Northwest"] naive = nw["overdose_deaths"].sum() / nw["population_2023"].sum() * 100_000 reported = nw.dropna(subset=["overdose_deaths"]) correct = reported["overdose_deaths"].sum() / reported["population_2023"].sum() * 100_000 print(f"Naive: {naive:.1f} per 100,000 (denominator: {len(nw)} counties)") print(f"Correct: {correct:.1f} per 100,000 (denominator: {len(reported)} counties)") print(f"Bias: {naive - correct:.1f}") 
import pandas as pd

overdose = pd.read_csv("data/pa_overdose_county_year.csv")
counties = pd.read_csv("data/pa_counties.csv")

o = overdose[overdose["year"] == 2023].merge(counties, on="county", how="left")
nw = o[o["region"] == "Northwest"]

naive = nw["overdose_deaths"].sum() / nw["population_2023"].sum() * 100_000

reported = nw.dropna(subset=["overdose_deaths"])
correct = reported["overdose_deaths"].sum() / reported["population_2023"].sum() * 100_000

print(f"Naive:   {naive:.1f} per 100,000  (denominator: {len(nw)} counties)")
print(f"Correct: {correct:.1f} per 100,000  (denominator: {len(reported)} counties)")
print(f"Bias:    {naive - correct:.1f}")

A silent error, largest where it matters most

No exception is raised and the naive figure looks reasonable. The bias runs downward and is largest in rural regions, which is exactly where suppression concentrates.

The rule: whenever a ratio is computed over a group, confirm the numerator and the denominator cover the same rows.

Exercise 7 — Reshape

Exercise 7 — Reshape

Worked example. .melt() turns a wide table into a long one: one row per county-year, with the year as data. .pivot() reverses it.

Exercise 7 — Reshape — your turn

Your turn. The overdose file arrives long: one row per county-year. Spread it wide, one row per county and one column per year, then fold it back. Read data/pa_overdose_county_year.csv as overdose. wide is overdose reshaped with .pivot(), using index="county", columns="year", and values="rate_per_100k". long is wide.reset_index() melted with id_vars="county", var_name="year", and value_name="rate_per_100k". Print the shape of each.

# The blank is pivot. It moves `county` into the index and spreads the six
# years across the columns: 67 rows by 6 columns.
# .reset_index() turns the index back into an ordinary column, which .melt()
# needs for id_vars. Folding back gives 67 x 6 = 402 rows, as in the file.
# Suppressed county-years stay blank in both shapes.
import pandas as pd overdose = pd.read_csv("data/pa_overdose_county_year.csv") wide = overdose.pivot(index="county", columns="year", values="rate_per_100k") print(wide.shape) print(wide.loc[["Allegheny", "Centre", "Schuylkill"]]) long = wide.reset_index().melt(id_vars="county", var_name="year", value_name="rate_per_100k") print(long.shape) 
import pandas as pd

overdose = pd.read_csv("data/pa_overdose_county_year.csv")

wide = overdose.pivot(index="county", columns="year", values="rate_per_100k")

print(wide.shape)
print(wide.loc[["Allegheny", "Centre", "Schuylkill"]])

Wide is for reading; long is for grouping

A row per county with the years across is easy to scan. .groupby("year") needs the year as a column of data, which only the long form has.

Exercise 8 — Dates — your turn

Your turn. Total COVID deaths by month across the state, then find the worst month. Read data/pa_covid_monthly.csv as covid and convert its month column with pd.to_datetime(). by_month is a Series of statewide totals, one per month: covid_deaths summed by month. peak_month is the month with the largest total (.idxmax()); peak_deaths is that total. Print both.

# The blank is to_datetime — a function on pd, not a method on the column.
# .idxmax() returns the INDEX LABEL of the largest value, where .max()
# returns the value itself. Here the index is the month, so idxmax gives a
# date. This is the same value-versus-position distinction as NumPy's
# .argmax() in Module 3.
# The peak is January 2022 — the Omicron wave — at 4,294 deaths.
import pandas as pd covid = pd.read_csv("data/pa_covid_monthly.csv") covid["month"] = pd.to_datetime(covid["month"]) by_month = covid.groupby("month")["covid_deaths"].sum() peak_month = by_month.idxmax() peak_deaths = by_month.max() print(f"Months covered: {by_month.size}") print(f"Worst month: {peak_month.date()} ({peak_deaths} deaths)") print() print(covid.groupby(covid["month"].dt.year)["covid_deaths"].sum()) 
import pandas as pd

covid = pd.read_csv("data/pa_covid_monthly.csv")
covid["month"] = pd.to_datetime(covid["month"])

by_month = covid.groupby("month")["covid_deaths"].sum()

peak_month = by_month.idxmax()
peak_deaths = by_month.max()

print(f"Months covered: {by_month.size}")
print(f"Worst month:    {peak_month.date()}  ({peak_deaths} deaths)")
print()
