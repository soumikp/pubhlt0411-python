The maternal and infant data

Source: County Health Rankings & Roadmaps 2024. Low birthweight is the percentage of live births under 2,500 grams, 5.3% (Union) to 11.0% (Philadelphia).

Metro counties have the higher median low-birthweight rate, 8.1% against 7.3%, even though child poverty is higher in nonmetro counties.

The question this module opens

Does child poverty account for the metro difference, or does the difference persist at the same level of poverty?

This is the cold open, and it is built to surprise. Most of the room expects rural counties to do worse on every maternal outcome, because nonmetro Pennsylvania is poorer: median child poverty 17.8% against 14.3%. And yet low birthweight runs the other way.

Do not explain it. The obvious guess is that the metro number is dragged up by Philadelphia, and Philadelphia is indeed the top county at 11.0%. But it is one county of thirty-seven, and a median does not move much for one county. The facet slides answer the question properly.

Framing, because this is maternal health: these are county rates, and differences between them reflect access to care, housing, income, and the structure of a place. Nothing on today’s slides is about the choices of individual mothers, and you should say so if anyone drifts there.

More geoms

sns.histplot() and sns.kdeplot()

A histogram counts rows in bins. A density curve is a smoothed outline of the same distribution, scaled so its area is 1.

binwidth= and bins= are settings: fixed numbers, not columns, so they never go to a mapping.

Two views of one column. The histogram counts counties in half-point bins; the density draws a smooth outline whose area is one, so the y-axis is no longer a count.

Read the shape. Most counties sit between six and nine percent, with a long right tail out to Philadelphia at eleven. The density makes the tail look gentler than the histogram does, which is the trade: smoothing hides the lumps and invents a little shape past the edges of the data.

The R half makes the same point about bins: it is not inside aes() because it is not data. Same here. binwidth=0.5 is a setting.

sns.boxplot() and sns.violinplot()

A violin draws the density of each group on both sides of a centre line, where a boxplot draws only its quartiles.

hue= repeats the x column so each box takes its colour from palette; legend=False drops the legend, which would only repeat the axis labels.

The boxplot is last week’s: median line, box for the middle half, whiskers to one and a half box lengths, and single points beyond. The metro box sits higher. That is the cold open again.

The violin shows what the box cannot. The metro violin has a long upper neck — that is Philadelphia and Fayette pulling up the tail — while the nonmetro violin is shorter and wider in the middle. Two groups with similar boxes can have very different violins, which is Rebecca’s point on the matching R slide.

The hue repeat is a seaborn 0.13 habit worth naming. Passing palette without hue still works but prints a warning that it will stop working. Map the same column to hue and switch the legend off.

seaborn’s three families

Every seaborn mark belongs to a family, and each family has a figure-level function that can split the data into panels.

Family Figure-level Axes-level marks
Relational: two numeric variables sns.relplot() scatterplot, lineplot
Distributional: one numeric variable sns.displot() histplot, kdeplot
Categorical: numeric by category sns.catplot() boxplot, violinplot, countplot, barplot

Axes-level functions draw on one ax=. Figure-level functions create their own figure and take col= and row= instead. The seaborn gallery shows a worked example of each.

This is the map for the rest of the session, and the Python counterpart of Rebecca’s “ggplot2 has more than forty geoms” slide. seaborn has far fewer, organised into three families.

The distinction that matters today is axes-level against figure-level. Everything so far has been axes-level: make the axes with plt.subplots(), pass ax=ax. The figure-level functions build the figure themselves, which is why they can make one panel per group. That is facets, and it is the middle of today.

The gallery link is for the final project. Many of the figures there are two or three calls.

Layers and statistical transformations

Layering: order matters

Each call draws on top of the previous one, so the layer that must be seen goes last.

zorder= overrides the order: a layer with a higher zorder is drawn in front.

Same two layers, opposite order. On the left the grey layer goes down last and paints over every metro county; the figure shows no metro counties at all, and the code ran without complaint. On the right the grey goes first and becomes a backdrop, and the metro counties stand out against the rest of the state.

This is Rebecca’s “order matters” slide. Her example layers a violin and a boxplot. In seaborn that pair does not show the problem, because seaborn gives its boxes and points a higher zorder than its violins, so they land in front whatever the code order. That is worth one sentence if someone tries it: the library made the choice for you.

xy is a dictionary of the arguments both calls share, unpacked with **. It keeps the calls from drifting apart, and it is the only new Python on the slide.

Statistical transformations

Many marks compute something before drawing. countplot counts rows; barplot computes a mean of y and adds an error bar by default.

errorbar=None removes the error bar. The interval it draws is inference, which this course does not cover.

Left and middle are the same picture by two routes. countplot does the counting; barplot draws whatever numbers it is handed. That is Rebecca’s stat = "count" against stat = "identity".

The right panel is the trap. Hand barplot a raw column and it silently averages it per group and adds a black line. Nobody asked for a mean, and the line is a 95% bootstrap confidence interval — inference, which this course has deliberately not taught. Students paste this from the web and report the bar as if it were a count.

The rule: know what the mark computed before reading it. For barplot on raw rows, add errorbar=None, and say “mean” in the axis label. Lab Question 6 is this slide.

One summary, or one per group

A mapping applies to every layer that receives it. A layer without hue= summarises all rows at once.

regplot computes a straight-line fit and draws it. ci=None turns off the shaded band, which is inference again.

Left: one line through all sixty-seven counties. The points are coloured by metro status, but the line ignores that, because the line layer never received hue. Rebecca’s R slide makes the same point with color inside or outside the shared aes().

Right: one line per group. Read the gap between them. At the same level of child poverty, the metro line sits above the nonmetro line. That is the first real answer to the cold open: poverty does not account for the difference, because the difference is still there when poverty is held level.

Careful with the language. This is a picture of county averages, not a model of anyone’s risk. “Held level” here means “compare counties with similar poverty”, nothing stronger.

Facets

Facets: one panel per group

col= gives each value of a column its own panel. col_wrap= wraps a long row of panels onto several lines.

relplot returns a FacetGrid, g, whose methods label every panel at once.

This is the alternative Module 5 promised for a scatter with too many colours. Six regions as six colours on one axes was unreadable; six panels is easy.

relplot is the figure-level version of scatterplot. No ax= — it makes the axes. col_wrap=3 is Rebecca’s facet_wrap: one variable, laid out in rows of three.

All six panels share both axes by default, so a point in the Northwest panel and a point in the Southeast panel are directly comparable. That default is usually right; the shared-axes slide returns to it.

The conditioning variable must be categorical. Facet on a numeric column and seaborn makes one panel per distinct value — fifty panels of one point each.

Facet grids: two conditioning variables

row= and col= together give one panel per combination. A numeric column becomes a condition once it is cut into groups.

This is Rebecca’s facet_grid(smoke ~ race). There is no second categorical column in this file that answers the cold open, so the slide makes one: above or below the statewide median of child poverty, 16.1%. np.where is Module 3.

Now read the printout, which is the cold open answered. Higher-poverty counties: metro 8.35, nonmetro 7.90. Lower-poverty counties: metro 7.70, nonmetro 6.40. Metro counties are higher in both columns. Poverty moves the rate, and the metro difference survives inside each poverty group.

Point at the cell sizes too. Only seven nonmetro counties are below the poverty median; twenty- seven metro counties are. A grid shows thin cells honestly, and a pooled figure hides them.

Facets versus aesthetics

A facet removes the other groups from view. Drawing all rows in grey behind each panel restores the comparison.

g.axes.flat reaches each panel’s matplotlib axes, so any matplotlib call can add to it.

Rebecca’s slide compares colour-and-shape on one panel against facets, and then adds the full data back. This slide goes straight to the useful version.

The grey points are every county; the blue points are the panel’s group. Now the comparison is inside each panel: the metro counties sit in the upper part of the grey cloud, the nonmetro counties in the lower part, at overlapping levels of poverty. That is the per-group line slide, seen a different way.

zorder controls which layer is in front, regardless of the order of the code. Higher is in front. It is the escape hatch when a library draws in an order you did not choose.

Rows or columns; shared axes

Panels stacked in a column share an x-axis, so their distributions line up. Panels side by side share a y-axis.

sharex=False or sharey=False gives each panel its own scale, at the cost of the comparison.

Choose the direction from the comparison. Here the question is where the two distributions sit along the low-birthweight scale, so the panels are stacked and the x-axes line up. The metro histogram is shifted right; read it straight down.

Side by side would line up the y-axes instead, which suits comparing heights — counts, or a response on the vertical axis.

Turning sharing off is occasionally necessary, when one group’s range dwarfs another’s. Do it knowingly: two panels on different scales look comparable and are not.

Position adjustments

Position adjustments: bars

When groups overlap in one bar, multiple= decides where each goes: stack, dodge (side by side), or fill (stack, scaled to proportions).

histplot counts a categorical column just as countplot does, and it accepts multiple=, which countplot does not. That is why it is used here.

Stack answers “how many counties per region, and how many of each kind”. Dodge makes the two groups easier to compare with each other. Fill throws away the totals and answers “what share of each region is metro”. Three questions, three adjustments.

Read fill: the Southeast is nearly all metro, the North Central and Northwest mostly nonmetro. This is the same picture of Pennsylvania as Module 5’s countplot, now as proportions.

Position adjustments: histograms

Stacked histograms hide the shape of every group except the bottom one. Layering with transparency, or drawing outlines with element="step", keeps each group fully visible.

The left panel looks like one distribution with a gold cap. The metro shape is there, but it starts at the top of the nonmetro bars, so nobody can read it. That is the trouble with stacking distributions.

Middle: both histograms from zero, see-through, so the overlap shows as a darker colour. Right: outlines only. Both keep each group whole. Rebecca’s version of this uses position = "identity" with alpha, and it is the same idea.

Lab Question 2 asks for a histogram where each group is fully visible. This is the slide.

Scales

Scales: ticks, labels, and log axes

A scale maps data values to positions. matplotlib sets ticks automatically; set_xscale("log") spaces values by ratio, and a locator fixes the tick spacing.

Population runs from about 4,400 in Cameron to 1.55 million in Philadelphia. On a linear axis, most counties crowd into the left tenth of the axis and a handful of large ones spread across the rest. On a log axis each step is ten times the last, and the counties spread out. Population, income, and counts nearly always want a log axis.

MultipleLocator(1) puts a tick at every whole percentage point. StrMethodFormatter adds the thousands commas. Both are matplotlib, reached through ax.xaxis or ax.yaxis — the axis, not the axes, from Module 5.

Rebecca’s R equivalent is scale_x_continuous(breaks = ...). The idea is the same; the spelling is different. Lab Question 3 uses MultipleLocator.

Colour scales: named and manual palettes

palette= takes either a named palette, such as the ColorBrewer set "Dark2", or a dictionary from each group to a colour.

sns.color_palette("Dark2") displays a named palette’s colours.

The named palette is a shortcut: seaborn hands out its colours in order. That is Rebecca’s scale_color_brewer(palette = "Dark2"). ColorBrewer is the source of most good named palettes, and colorbrewer2.org lets you browse them.

The dictionary is Rebecca’s scale_color_manual, and it is the version to use in anything that leaves a notebook. Metro is royal blue in every figure, whatever order the rows arrive in. With a named palette, filter the data and the colours can shift between two figures in the same report.

Lab Question 3 asks for two named colours by group. The dictionary is how.

Diverging colour scales: a correlation heatmap

A diverging palette has a neutral centre and two directions, which suits a value with a meaningful zero.

vmin=-1, vmax=1 fixes the colour scale. Without it the colours stretch to whatever range is present, and the same colour means different numbers in two heatmaps.

BrBG is brown–blue-green, diverging around white at zero. A sequential palette such as viridis would make −0.8 and +0.2 look like two steps of one ladder, when they point in opposite directions.

Read across the low-birthweight row: child poverty at +0.47, and uninsured children at −0.50 — a negative that runs against intuition. Do not explain it away; it is a good example of why a county correlation is a description, not a mechanism. The strongest cell on the whole map is income against poverty, −0.80, and that one is nearly a definition, not a finding. .corr() drops missing values pair by pair, so the infant-mortality cells use 44 counties and the rest use 66 or 67.

Themes

Built-in styles

A theme sets the non-data elements: background, grid, and ticks. seaborn has five styles.

sns.set_theme(style="white") sets the style for every later figure in the session.

These are Rebecca’s theme_bw(), theme_minimal(), theme_classic(). Same data, same marks, four different backgrounds.

white and ticks are the usual choices for print: no grey, no grid competing with the data. whitegrid helps when readers need to read values off the axis.

The with block is a technical detail worth one sentence. sns.set_theme changes the style for everything that follows, and in a browser deck that would leak into every later slide. with sns.axes_style(...) applies it to one axes only. In a notebook, call set_theme once at the top.

Adjusting single elements

Individual elements change through matplotlib and two seaborn helpers: sns.despine() and sns.move_legend().

legend=False in the plotting call removes the legend entirely.

This is Rebecca’s theme() slide: legend on top, no legend title, a bolder title.

despine removes the top and right borders, which carry no information. move_legend is the reliable way to reposition a seaborn legend; bbox_to_anchor=(0.5, 1.0) means centred, at the top edge of the axes. pad=28 lifts the title so the legend fits beneath it.

Lab Question 3 is the themes exercise from the R half: colours by group, tick spacing, an axis title, a plain background, no legend, and a bold title. Every one of those calls is on this slide or the two before it.

Combining and saving

Combining figures

plt.subplot_mosaic() lays out named panels from a sketch: each list is a row, and a repeated name spans several cells.

This is Rebecca’s patchwork slide, rebuilt. (hist | dens) / violin in patchwork is the nested list here: two names in the top row, one name repeated across the bottom row so it spans both columns.

axd is a dictionary from panel name to axes, so axd["vio"] is the bottom panel. Name panels by what they hold and the code reads like the layout.

plt.subplots(2, 2) from Module 5 is the simple grid; subplot_mosaic is for anything uneven. fig.suptitle titles the whole figure, where ax.set_title titles one panel.

Saving figures

fig.savefig() writes the figure to a file. The extension chooses the format.

savefig() comes before plt.show(): after showing, the figure may be empty.

PNG for slides and the web; PDF for papers, because it is drawn from shapes and stays sharp at any zoom. dpi=300 is the usual print resolution. bbox_inches="tight" trims empty margins and stops long labels being clipped.

The order rule catches people every term. In a notebook, plt.show() can hand the figure off and leave nothing to save, and the result is a blank file with no error.

In the browser the files go to a temporary in-memory folder and vanish on refresh. In JupyterLab they appear next to the notebook. Rebecca also recommends dating file names and saving into an output/ folder; that habit is worth copying.

Appendix: why visualize?

Identical summaries, different data

Anscombe’s quartet (1973): four data sets with the same means, variances, and correlation.

This is Rebecca’s appendix point, with Anscombe’s four sets instead of the datasaurus. Every printed line is the same: mean 7.5, standard deviation 2.03, correlation 0.816. The four pictures are a line, a curve, a line with one outlier, and a vertical stack with one point carrying the entire correlation.

The values are typed in from Anscombe’s 1973 paper. They are constructed, not public-health data, and that is the point: they were built to show that a summary can be identical while the data are not.

Tie it back to Module 5. The asthma correlation of 0.659 was reported after its scatter, never before. This is why.

A figure of counts often draws a map of population

The two lists share no counties. Deaths track population at r = +0.953; the rate does not.

Ask for predictions first. Most of the room expects overlap. There is none.

Left: Allegheny, Philadelphia, Montgomery, Lancaster, Bucks — Pennsylvania’s largest counties. That chart is a population map with a mortality label; deaths and population correlate at +0.953. Right: Potter, Juniata, Jefferson, Armstrong, Fulton — small rural counties. Potter leads at 556 per 100,000, from 89 deaths. Divide by population and the correlation with population falls to −0.308.

Both charts are correct for different questions. Total deaths answers how many beds or staff; the rate answers where the burden per resident was highest. One caution on the rate: Potter’s 556 is 89 deaths in a county of 16,000, and a small count makes a jumpy rate.
